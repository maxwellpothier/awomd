This week, Pitchfork dropped their list of the
[100 Best Indie Rock Albums of All Time](https://pitchfork.com/story/the-100-best-indie-rock-albums-of-all-time/),
so unfortunately for me, I felt the need to go deep on every album in there. I made some decent progress.

Now, you might ask, what even is indie rock? I don't really know. And now that
I'm looking at the list again, they also pose the question.

The way I'd define it, combined with the Pitchfork definition, is rock music
that pushes the boundaries of what rock is, maybe by dabbling in other genres or
using different instruments or production techniques. Very vague. Then
Pitchfork added the qualification that it must be released by an indie label
(literally any label other than Sony, Universal, and Warner). It gets trickier
when there are dozens of labels who market themselves as "indie" but really
they're just a subsidiary of one of the majors.

I think the indie-label qualification is important, though. You've got to have
a lot more grit to make it big on the indie path, with much less funding and
marketing, and a *lot* more risk. The indie rock albums that made it big here
had to take big swings, and lots of times that leads to more adventurous music.

The album that's been sticking with me the most, from the list, is this one:

It comes in at #13 (wow! I was thinking it was in the top half of the list till
I just checked again). I hadn't listened to this before, and the placement is
hard to argue with, honestly. It's slow, haunting, the perfect album for an
overcast fall, maybe slightly rainy, day.

The craziest thing to point out here: listening to it, it sounds *exactly* like
the indie folk/indie pop from the early 2010s. I can't believe this came out in
'98.

My favorite song is
**[Metal Heart](https://open.spotify.com/track/4JeMpERu0LS3QhNoNG0cvZ)**. It
starts with a plucking electric guitar, layered vocals, and lyrics that are
slipping away in real time. It takes its sweet time just filling up space
before the drums roll in, all building to a very resolute, hooky chorus. The
meaning of the song could go a million different directions.

**[Cross Bones Style](https://open.spotify.com/track/5Qhy5MYSkAMRVgTrABOlp7)**
and **[American Flag](https://open.spotify.com/track/6F8gDaDl7ksWnv0Z7mwN53)**
are more rhythm-forward. Cross Bones Style puts a steady and driving drum fill
under the hypnotic guitar line that makes it sound even more modern. I would
kill to have seen this one live.

American Flag is an unexpected opener. Apparently Chan (Cat) wanted more texture on
the song, so she brought a copy of
[Paul Revere](https://open.spotify.com/track/3gKwVWwKmeuFtPubICbOGc) by the
Beastie Boys to the studio and had its drumbeat reversed and mixed straight
through the middle of the song. It sounds really different from the rest of the
album, but it works, and it sets the tone well.

**[Colors and the Kids](https://open.spotify.com/track/7hho5rPdTz9FRQfs6uW7kF)**
is on the other side of the spectrum. It's just Chan and a piano. The
lyrics feel monolithic, like I need to sit with the song for a long time and
let them, and the way they're sung, soak in. Generally it feels like it's about
learning to return to simplicity to find fulfillment and happiness when life
gets complicated and confusing.

The album is fantastic, way ahead of its time, and certainly feels unlike
anything I've listened to before.
</Album>

Also, you should check out
[Pitchfork's list](https://pitchfork.com/story/the-100-best-indie-rock-albums-of-all-time/)
to see if anything piques your interest, or if there's anything there you
recognize. They did a good job with it, imo!
